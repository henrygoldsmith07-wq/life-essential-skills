# 06 · Critical thinking and checking information

[Curriculum](../README.md) · [Next: Digital safety](07-digital-safety.md)

**Goal:** distinguish a claim from its evidence and make a decision with your uncertainty visible.

## Learn

Turn a vague claim into a specific question. "This course guarantees success" needs clarification: what outcome, for whom, over what period, and compared with what alternative?

Find the original source, check its date and context, and ask whether it actually supports the claim. A confident speaker, a polished chart, or many shares is not proof. Check independent sources and look for the strongest reasonable counterargument. AI-generated answers also need verification, especially for important decisions.

Be careful with numbers. A percentage needs a denominator: an increase from 1 in 1,000 to 2 in 1,000 doubles the relative rate but changes the absolute rate by 1 in 1,000. An association does not by itself establish that one thing caused another. Ask what else might explain the result.

For a decision, write down your options, priorities, evidence, assumptions, and what would change your mind. This makes learning from the result easier.

## Example

A supplement advertisement says "twice as effective." You cannot assess it without knowing effective at what, compared with what, how measured, and whether the study applies to you. Look for the original evidence and ask a qualified clinician about personal health decisions.

## Practise

1. Choose one claim from an advertisement or social post.
2. Write the exact claim and find its original source.
3. Note the date, population, measure, limitations, and conflicts of interest.
4. Compare with an independent reliable source.
5. Record your conclusion and uncertainty in the [decision journal](../templates/decision-journal.md).

## Self-check

1. What is missing from "risk doubled"?
2. Why might two variables move together without one causing the other?
3. What evidence would change your conclusion?

<details>
<summary>Suggested answers</summary>

1. The starting risk, population, time period, and how risk was measured.
2. Another factor may affect both, or the apparent relationship may reflect selection or chance.
3. Specify relevant evidence rather than treating your first belief as permanent.

</details>

**Done when:** you can explain a claim, the evidence behind it, and at least one limitation.

**Teaching note:** examples are hypothetical reasoning exercises. They do not describe a particular study or product.
